problem: "Our recordings capture young children and their families at home, including bystanders who never signed anything, in up to 40 countries. Each country has different rules, CNRS had no precedent for this kind of data, and releasing an AI model trained on it raised new questions: could it leak information about the families, and who should be allowed to use it? Research teams needed practical answers, not legal texts."
constraints:
  - "GDPR plus national legislation in every country where data was collected"
  - "Community data sovereignty: some data came with requirements set by Aboriginal leadership"
  - "European Research Council ethics review of novel AI methods"
  - "Safeguards had to protect families without making the data useless for science"
approach: "I worked with institutional Data Protection Officers to design data-management plans and Data Protection Impact Assessments for research with children and caregivers, then turned those requirements into operational workflows across the data lifecycle: collection, annotation, storage, analysis and reuse. I co-authored a handbook that reviews legislation in 28 countries and turns it into practical guidance for teams collecting this type of data. During the ERC ethics review, I acted as translator between ethics reviewers and technical staff and set up an ad hoc ethics board. For BabyHuBERT, I led the governance arrangements, including a licensing approach to manage and reduce the risks of sharing a model trained on child-centred data."

keyDecisions:
  - decision: "Release BabyHuBERT under a licence with conditions, rather than fully open or not at all"
    reasoning: "Fully open weights would maximise reuse but give no recourse against misuse of a model trained on children's voices. Not releasing it would waste a public investment and slow down the field. A licence keeps the model useful while setting clear limits."
    alternatives:
      - "Fully open release"
      - "Keep the model internal"
      - "Share only through individual collaborations"
  - decision: "Write country-by-country practical guidance instead of applying one global policy"
    reasoning: "Applying the strictest regime everywhere blocks legitimate work, while leaving each team to work it out alone produces inconsistent and risky practice."
    alternatives:
      - "Apply GDPR-level rules everywhere"
      - "Leave compliance to each local team"
  - decision: "Let community requirements shape access, not just the law"
    reasoning: "Legal compliance is the floor. Trust from the communities who share their data is what makes the work possible in the long run."
